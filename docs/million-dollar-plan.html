Before the numbers: what this document is and isn't

No business plan makes a million dollars — execution does. Every figure here is internally consistent: the crew count supports the job count, the job count supports the revenue, the revenue supports the payroll. They are still assumptions. Replace each one with your own market's data the moment you have it.

Two things worth being blunt about. This is a five-season plan, not a fast one — anyone selling a million-dollar first year in a trade with ninety days of revenue is selling something else. And $1.07M of revenue is roughly $183k of operating profit, not a million dollars of income. By then you have a warehouse, four trucks and a year-round payroll.

01

Five seasons, and the year the margin dips

Growth is roughly 55–60% a year from Season 3. That's demanding but ordinary for this trade — what isn't ordinary is what happens to margin in Season 4.

Read the margin, not the revenue. Gross margin climbs to 64% by Season 3 and then falls to 56%. That isn't an error. Seasons 1–3 are owner-operated with almost no fixed overhead, and part of that "margin" is your own unpaid labor. Seasons 4–5 add a warehouse, year-round salaries and crews you pay whether or not it rains.

Season 4 is the hardest year in this plan. You take on the full cost structure a season before the renewal base is large enough to carry it. Profit falls from $168k to $132k on nearly 60% more revenue. Budget for the dip — and do not respond to it by cutting marketing, because that dip is precisely what buys Season 5.

02

What a million-dollar season is made of

456 jobs across four revenue lines. They are not equally valuable, and the biggest one is not the most important one.

Renewals — 25% of revenue at ~69% margin. 215 customers who cost you nothing to win, because you are storing their custom-cut lights in your warehouse, labeled by address. This line is the entire reason the business is worth building; everything else exists to feed it.

Commercial — 25%, and the most controllable growth in the plan. It books in August, pays on contract and renews without a sales cycle. Going from 24 accounts to 42 is outbound work by one person, not an advertising bet.

Permanent architectural lighting — 10%, installed in the off-season. Track-mounted year-round LED at a $7,800 average, sold to customers who already trust you on their roof. It consumes zero peak capacity, which is what makes it the line that de-risks a ninety-day business.

New residential — 37%, and your most expensive revenue. Necessary fuel for the renewal base, but it carries 26% material cost against a renewal's 10%, plus roughly $250 of acquisition cost each.

A $430k business becomes a $1M business mostly by keeping customers and signing contracts — not by advertising harder.

03

Does six crews actually cover it?

This is where scale plans usually lie. It checks out — but only at six crews, and only if you stop selling at 90% of capacity.

A two-person crew installs 2.5 standard homes a day. The install window — October 15 to December 20 — is about 45 working days, so one crew is worth 112 home-equivalents a season.

January is the check that makes it work. 400 residential takedowns at 5 homes per crew-day is 80 crew-days, plus about 30 for commercial. Across the 21 working days of January that's 5.2 crews — the same six, retained.

That is why takedown is the strategy rather than a chore. It lets you offer installers a fifteen-week job instead of an eleven-week one, which is what lets you hire good people and get them back next year. Nobody good leaves a year-round job for eleven weeks.

Headcount at a million: 12 seasonal installers, one year-round operations manager, one year-round sales estimator, a part-time admin, and you. Four trucks, six trailers, one 4,000 sq ft warehouse.

05

Cash, and the September problem

You spend about $310,000 before the first Season-5 dollar lands. This is where profitable companies in this trade actually die.

Pre-revenue outflow
OutflowWhenAmount
Inventory buy, ahead of the seasonal markupJune–July$145,000
Insurance renewalJuly$22,000
Marketing, front-loadedAug–Oct$65,000
Payroll before deposits clearSept–Oct$48,000
Warehouse and fixed overhead, off-seasonMar–Sep$30,000
Total$310,000

1. Fifty percent deposits. 456 jobs at half of a ~$2,350 average ticket, landing August through October, is the primary engine. No exceptions, no "we'll invoice after the install." This single policy is the difference between needing a bank and not.

2. Commercial signed in August, half on signature. 42 accounts × $3,250 is about $136,000 in the door before the peak marketing budget goes out.

3. A $150,000 line of credit, arranged in February — when your books look their best, not in September when you need it. Draw it only for the inventory buy and repay it by December 31. Arrange it even if you never intend to use it.

The failure mode, stated plainly

Growing from $680k to $1.07M means buying roughly $100,000 more inventory in July on the belief that you'll sell it in October. Do not buy Season-5 inventory against Season-5 hopes. Buy it against signed commercial contracts and closed renewals, and buy the remainder in September at a worse price. A 25% markup on the last tranche is cheap next to $100,000 of unsold C9 sitting on a shelf until next November.

06

The two-year sequence

Every hire and every commitment lands an off-season early, on purpose. Hiring an operations manager in October is hiring them too late to matter.

S4 · February
Arrange the line of credit. Rewrite pricing from Season 4's real per-job margins, and kill the job types that lost money.
S4 · March
Hire the operations manager. They need a full off-season to own the calendar before it matters.
S4 · Mar–Sep
Launch permanent architectural lighting to the existing list. Eight jobs. It funds the ops manager and proves the off-season model.
S4 · June
Sign the warehouse. You cannot label and shelve 400 customer kits in a 10×20 storage unit.
S4 · August
Run the renewal campaign like a product launch, not an email. Target 75% of renewals closed before Labor Day.
S5 · February
Hire the sales estimator. Their whole Q2–Q3 job is commercial outbound: 24 accounts to 42.
S5 · June–July
The inventory buy — against contracts, never against forecasts.
S5 · July–Sept
Recruit twelve installers. Rehire last year's crew first with a return bonus and let them recruit their friends.
S5 · October
Stop selling at 90% of capacity. Put the rest on the Season-6 list. A waitlist is an asset; a missed install is a one-star review.
S5 · January
Six crews, 21 days, 400 takedowns. This month is the reason next year's renewals renew.
07

What breaks this plan

Ranked by expected damage, with the arithmetic. The order is not the order most operators worry in.

01

The renewal rate slips

Catastrophic

The plan assumes 72%. At 55%: renewals fall from 215 to 164, a direct loss of $63,750. To hold 400 residential jobs you must then acquire 51 more new customers at ~$250 each ($12,750 of marketing) — and they carry 26% material cost instead of 10%, another $28,000 of COGS.

Total impact: roughly −$104,000, or 57% of your operating profit. It is far cheaper to keep a customer than to grow past losing one. And the renewal rate is set in January, not August: a clean, on-time takedown and a labeled kit is the retention program. Measure it every August; treat anything under 65% as a five-alarm fire.

02

You sell more than six crews can install

Severe · self-inflicted

The most common way this business fails publicly. Every over-sold install becomes a December emergency, a rushed job, a one-star review — and a lost renewal, which feeds failure 01.

Book to 85%. Cap it in October and say so out loud: "we're full — want the Season 6 list?" is the best marketing you will ever run.

03

You can't hire twelve competent installers

Likely

At six crews, labor is the binding constraint — not demand. Every mitigation happens in July, and none of them work in October.

Rehire last season's crew first with a return bonus. Recruit from roofing, landscaping and window-cleaning crews entering their off-season. Pay 15% above the local handyman rate. And sell the January takedown work as what it is: a fifteen-week job, not an eleven-week one.

04

A serious injury

Existential

Six crews at height, in the cold, under December schedule pressure. This is the risk that ends companies and hurts people.

$2M general liability plus workers' comp. Harnesses above 6/12 pitch or any second story, stabilizers on every ladder, no work in rain, ice or sustained wind over 20 mph, a documented daily brief, and a photograph of every ladder setup. Enforce the weather rule hardest when you are behind schedule — that is exactly when it gets broken, and exactly when it matters.

05

The July inventory bet goes wrong

Severe · cash

$145,000 of lights bought four months before the revenue arrives, in a business where an unsold strand is dead money until next November.

Buy against signed contracts and closed renewals only. Pay the September markup on the rest.

06

Weather compresses the window

Certain

Not a risk — a certainty. You will lose six to ten days, and every one of them is unrecoverable.

It is already priced in, as the 85% booking cap and the December 8–15 float. If you book to 100%, you have removed the mitigation and adopted the risk.

07

A national franchise enters your metro

Moderate

They will outspend you on advertising and undercut you on price. They cannot beat you on the January 15 takedown, the 24-hour fix, or knowing the customer's roof.

Do not match their price. Defend the renewal base — it is the one thing their marketing budget cannot buy.

Past a million

Six crews in one metro with one owner is roughly the ceiling. Three ways through it, in ascending order of risk.

Options beyond Season 5
PathWhat it meansRisk
DeepenPermanent lighting, gutter cleaning and washing sold to the list you already own. Same customers, more revenue each, year-round crews.Lowest. No new acquisition, no new market. Start here.
WidenA second metro, a second warehouse, a second operations manager.High. Everything that is hard at $1M is harder remotely.

You own a list of several hundred homeowners who have let you on their roof and trusted you with their storage. That relationship is the asset. Sell more to the customers you already have before you go find new ones.
