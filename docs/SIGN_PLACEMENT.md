# Where to put yard signs — Montgomery County, PA

Service radius: ~15 miles centered on the Wyndmoor / Chestnut Hill / Flourtown area.

---

## ⚠ Read this first: roadside signs are illegal in PA, and this is not a gray area

**You cannot legally plant a sign at an intersection, on a median, on a utility pole,
or along a highway shoulder.** Two separate layers of law prohibit it:

**State.** Section 425 of the PA State Highway Law prohibits signs within the state
highway right-of-way. PennDOT removes them **without notice** — they are not obligated
to warn you or return the sign. The right-of-way is wider than people assume: it
typically extends well past the pavement edge, often to the far side of the sidewalk or
utility poles. If you can see the sign from the road, you are probably inside it.

**Local.** Montgomery County townships permit contractor signs **only on the premises
where the work is being performed** — one per property, removed when the job is done.
An Everglow sign at a gas station where you have never worked is an *off-premise
commercial sign*, which is a different, permitted category almost everywhere, and
prohibited outright in many districts.

Every one of the pikes you would naturally target — Bethlehem, Germantown, Ridge,
Butler, Skippack, Old York, Easton, Welsh — is a state route. That is the whole list of
good corridors, and it is exactly the list PennDOT patrols.

**What it actually costs you:** signs vanish within days on the busiest roads (that is
where enforcement looks first), so you pay $6 a sign to feed a truck. Worse, the local
Facebook groups in Chestnut Hill, Ambler and Blue Bell are merciless about bandit
signs, and you are selling a premium, trust-led service to exactly those people.

**So the strategy below is permission-based.** Same corridors, same traffic, legal, and
the sign is still standing in December.

## The three placements that actually work

Ranked by leads per sign, best first.

### 1. Every house you light — this is the whole game

Your own plan budgets **1 lead per 6–8 signs**, and that number comes from job-site
signs. It works because of proof: the sign is standing in front of a house you actually
lit, on a street where the neighbors have already noticed. A sign at a gas station has
no proof behind it and converts at a small fraction of that rate.

Ask during the walkthrough, not after: *"Mind if we leave a small sign by the driveway
through the season? We'll pull it at takedown."* Roughly 70% say yes.

This is on-premise work signage, explicitly allowed. **Do this everywhere before you
spend one hour on anything below.**

### 2. Host properties on the busy corridors — where your list goes

A business fronting a high-traffic pike, whose owner lets you put a sign on their
**private** lot. Legal with permission, and you get the corridor traffic you are after.

The trade: you need a reason for them to say yes. Three that work —
- **Light their storefront free or at cost.** Now it is on-premise signage for a real
  job, which is the cleanest possible footing, and their lit building is your billboard.
- **Referral fee.** $50–100 per closed job traced to their lot.
- **Reciprocity.** Their flyers in your customer thank-you cards.

The first one is strongest by a distance. A commercial install you do at cost in
mid-November, on a corridor with 20,000 cars a day, is the best advertising buy in
this whole business plan — and your own lead-gen doc already says commercial books in
August–September, so you have the opening.

### 3. Neighborhood entrances — with the HOA's written OK

Some HOAs and civic associations allow a sponsor sign at the entrance, especially if
you light the entrance monument for free. Ask the association, in writing, not the
nearest homeowner.

## The permission script

Walk in mid-morning on a weekday. Ask for the owner or manager by title, not by name.

> "Hi — I run Everglow, we do holiday lighting in Flourtown and Chestnut Hill. I'd like
> to light the front of your building this season at my cost, and in exchange leave one
> small sign by your entrance through December. Your building looks good from the road
> at night, I get one sign, and I'll take it all down in January. Can I show you what it
> would look like?"

Get it **in writing** — even a text message. Two lines is enough: what you're placing,
where, and that it comes out by a set date. It protects you if a manager changes.

## Field worksheet — fill this in while driving, not from a map

I cannot verify signal timing, sight lines, or current tenancy from here. Nobody
publishes traffic-light cycle lengths. Drive the Tier A list once, in the dark, around
5:30–6:30pm on a weekday — peak traffic and the hour your product is visible.

For each node, record:

- [ ] **Red-light dwell** — time it with a stopwatch. Under 45 seconds is weak; 90+ is gold.
- [ ] **Which side of the road** stopped traffic actually faces. A sign behind the
      driver is worth nothing.
- [ ] **Sight line** — can you read a 1.8in phone number from the stop bar? Stand there.
- [ ] **Setback** — is there private lot frontage, or is it all right-of-way? If there is
      no private ground between the sidewalk and the building, skip it.
- [ ] **Who owns it** — independent operator (can decide today) or a chain (needs
      corporate, usually a no).
- [ ] **Lighting potential** — would their building actually look good lit? That is your
      offer.

Rank by dwell time × traffic, and work the top 8. Eight good host properties beat
twenty-five mediocre ones, and you will not have time for more in November anyway.

ask one question: *"Do I need a permit for an off-premise commercial sign on private
property?"* It is a five-minute call and it is the difference between a sign that stays
up and a citation.
