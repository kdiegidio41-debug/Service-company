# Everglow Holiday Lighting Co. — The Road to $1,000,000

*A scale plan that starts where `BUSINESS_PLAN.md` ends. That plan takes you to
**$430,000 in Season 3** on 3–4 crews. This one takes you from there to a
**$1.07M Season 5** — and is explicit about the three things that will break it.*

---

## 0. Read this part first

**No plan makes a million dollars. Execution does.** Every number below is
internally consistent — the crew count supports the job count, the job count
supports the revenue, the revenue supports the payroll — but they are
*assumptions*, not forecasts. Replace each one with your own data the moment
you have it.

Three specific honesty checks on this document:

1. **It is a five-season plan, not a fast one.** Anyone selling you $1M in
   year one in a 90-day-revenue trade is selling you something else.
2. **$1.07M of revenue is ~$183k of operating profit**, not $1M of income.
   At this scale you have a warehouse, four trucks, and a year-round payroll.
   Revenue is vanity; §5 is the real number.
3. **The single point of failure is the renewal rate.** §7 shows what happens
   when it slips, and it is brutal. Protect it above growth.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| — of which renewals | 0 | 38 | 76 | 130 | **215** |
| Commercial accounts | 6 | 14 | 24 | 34 | **42** |
| Permanent-lighting installs | 0 | 0 | 3 | 8 | **14** |
| Crews (peak) | 1 | 2 | 4 | 5 | **6** |
| Year-round staff (excl. owner) | 0 | 0 | 1 | 2 | **3** |
| **Revenue** | $130k | $258k | $430k | $680k | **$1.07M** |
| Gross margin | 54% | 61% | 64% | 58% | **56%** |
| **Operating profit** | $42k | $96k | $168k | $132k | **$183k** |

**Read the margin line carefully.** It goes *down* after Season 3. That is not
an error — it is the cost of becoming a real company. Seasons 1–3 are owner-
operated with no fixed overhead; the "margin" is partly your unpaid labor.
Seasons 4–5 add a warehouse, year-round salaries, and crews you pay whether or
not it rains. **Season 4 is the hardest year in this plan: you take on the full
cost structure before the renewal base is big enough to carry it.** Expect
profit to dip. Budget for it. Do not panic and cut marketing — that dip is
what buys Season 5.

## 2. What a $1,000,000 season is made of

| Line | Units | Avg ticket | Revenue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Residential — **renewals** | 215 | $1,250 | **$268,750** |
| Residential — new core | 155 | $1,750 | $271,250 |
| Residential — estate | 30 | $4,200 | $126,000 |
| Commercial contracts | 42 | $6,500 | $273,000 |
| Permanent architectural lighting | 14 | $7,800 | $109,200 |
| Service calls, add-ons, mid-season upsells | — | — | $25,000 |
| **Total** | **456 jobs** | | **$1,073,200** |

### The four levers, ranked by how much they matter

1. **Renewals (25% of revenue, ~69% margin).** 215 customers who cost you
   nothing to acquire. This is the entire reason the business is worth
   building. Everything else is in service of growing this number.
2. **Commercial (25% of revenue).** Books in August, pays on contract, renews
   without a sales cycle, and fills the calendar before residential starts.
   Going from 24 to 42 accounts is the most *controllable* growth in the plan —
   it's outbound, not advertising.
3. **Permanent architectural lighting (10% of revenue).** Track-mounted
   year-round LED, $7,800 average. Sold to customers who already trust you on
   their roof. Installed in the **off-season**, which is why it doesn't consume
   peak capacity. This is the line that de-risks a 90-day business.
4. **New residential (37% of revenue, lowest margin).** Necessary fuel for
   lever #1, but it is the *most expensive* revenue you sell. Growth here is
   capped by marketing spend and crew capacity, not demand.

> **The strategic point:** a $430k business grows to $1M mostly by *keeping*
> customers and *adding contracts*, not by advertising harder.

## 3. Does the capacity math actually work?

This is where scale plans usually lie. It checks out, but only with 6 crews.

**Install season (Oct 15 – Dec 20, ~45 working days), 2-person crew at 2.5
standard homes/day = 112 home-equivalents per crew-season.**

| Work | Volume | Home-equivalents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard residential (renewals + new core) | 370 | 370 (renewals are faster; call it even) |
| Estate | 30 | 75 (2.5× a standard home) |
| Commercial | 42 | 63 crew-days |
| **Total demand** | | **508 crew-day-equivalents** |

At **85% booked capacity** (the weather buffer — you *will* lose 6–10 days):
508 ÷ 0.85 = **598 crew-days needed ÷ 112 = 5.3 crews → staff 6.**

**Takedown (Jan 2–31, ~21 working days), 5 homes/day/crew:**
400 residential ÷ 5 = 80 crew-days, plus ~30 for commercial = **110 crew-days
÷ 21 days = 5.2 crews.** The same six crews, retained through January. This is
exactly why takedown is the strategy and not a chore — it's what lets you
offer a 15-week job instead of an 11-week one, which is what lets you hire
good people and get them back next year.

**Permanent lighting: 14 jobs × 3 crew-days = 42 crew-days, done Mar–Sep.**
Zero peak-season cost.

**Headcount at $1M:** 12 seasonal installers, 1 year-round operations manager,
1 year-round sales/estimator, 1 part-time admin, plus the owner. Four trucks,
six trailers, one 4,000 sq ft warehouse.

## 5. Cash, and the September problem

**At $1M, you spend roughly $310,000 before your first Season-5 dollar lands:**

| Pre-revenue outflow | When | Amount |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inventory buy (before the seasonal markup) | June–July | $145,000 |
| Insurance renewal | July | $22,000 |
| Marketing, front-loaded | Aug–Oct | $65,000 |
| Payroll before deposits clear | Sept–Oct | $48,000 |
| Warehouse + fixed overhead, off-season | Mar–Sep | $30,000 |
| **Total** | | **$310,000** |

**Where it comes from, in order of preference:**

1. **50% deposits.** 456 jobs × 50% of ~$2,350 average, front-loaded into
   Aug–Oct, is the primary engine. Non-negotiable, no exceptions, no
   "we'll invoice you after." This one policy is the difference between
   needing a bank and not.
2. **Commercial contracts signed in August**, 50% on signature. 42 accounts ×
   $3,250 = ~$136,000 landing before you spend the peak marketing budget.
3. **A $150,000 line of credit**, arranged in **February** when your books look
   their best — not in September when you need it. Draw it only for the
   inventory buy; repay by December 31. If you have never carried debt in this
   business, arrange the line anyway and don't use it.

> **The failure mode:** growing from $680k to $1.07M means buying ~$100k more
> inventory in July on the *belief* that you'll sell it in October. Profitable
> companies die here. Do not buy Season-5 inventory against Season-5 hopes —
> buy it against **signed commercial contracts and closed renewals**, and buy
> the rest in September at a worse price. Paying a 25% markup on the last
> tranche is cheap compared to $100k of unsold C9 on a shelf.

## 6. Season 4 → Season 5: what you actually do

| When | The one thing |
| --- | --- |
| **Season 4, February** | Arrange the line of credit. Rewrite pricing from Season 4's real per-job margins. Fire the job types that lost money. |
| **Season 4, March** | **Hire the operations manager.** Not in October. They need a full off-season to own the calendar before it matters. |
| **Season 4, Mar–Sep** | Launch permanent architectural lighting to your existing list. 8 jobs. This funds the ops manager and proves the off-season model. |
| **Season 4, June** | Sign the warehouse. Move out of the storage units — you cannot label and shelve 400 kits in a 10×20. |
| **Season 4, August** | Run the renewal campaign like a product launch, not an email. Target **75% of renewals closed before Labor Day.** |
| **Season 5, February** | Hire the sales/estimator. Their entire Q2–Q3 job is commercial outbound: 24 → 42 accounts. |
| **Season 5, June–July** | The inventory buy, against contracts (§5). |
| **Season 5, September** | **Recruit 12 installers.** Start in July. Rehire last year's crew first, pay them a return bonus, and let them recruit their friends. |
| **Season 5, October** | Stop selling at 90% of capacity. Put the rest on the Season-6 list. A waitlist is an asset; a missed install is a one-star review. |
| **Season 5, January** | Six crews, 21 days, 400 takedowns. This month is why the renewals renew. |

## 7. What breaks this plan

Ranked by expected damage, with the actual arithmetic.

### 1. The renewal rate slips (catastrophic)

Plan assumes **72%**. At **55%**:

- Renewals fall 215 → 164. Direct revenue loss: **$63,750.**
- To hold 400 residential jobs you must acquire **51 more new customers** at
  ~$250 CAC = **$12,750 more marketing**, and they carry 26% material cost
  instead of 10% — another **$28,000** of COGS.
- **Total profit impact: roughly −$104,000, or 57% of your operating profit.**

*It is far cheaper to keep a customer than to grow past losing them.* The
renewal rate is set in January, not August: a clean, on-time takedown and a
labeled kit is the retention program. Measure renewals every August and treat
anything under 65% as a five-alarm fire.

### 2. You sell more than six crews can install (severe, and self-inflicted)

The most common way this business fails *publicly*. Book to 85%. Cap it in
October. Every over-sold install becomes a December emergency, a rushed job, a
one-star review, and a lost renewal — which feeds failure #1.

### 3. You can't hire 12 competent installers (likely)

At 6 crews, labor is the binding constraint, not demand. Mitigations, all of
which happen in **July**: rehire last season's crew first with a return bonus,
recruit from roofing/landscaping/window-cleaning crews entering their
offseason, pay 15% above the local handyman rate, and offer the January
takedown work as a *15-week* job rather than an 11-week one. Nobody good
leaves a year-round job for eleven weeks.

### 4. A serious injury (existential)

Six crews at height, in the cold, under December schedule pressure. This is the
risk that ends companies and hurts people. $2M GL + workers' comp, harnesses
above 6/12 pitch or any second story, stabilizers on every ladder, no work in
rain/ice/wind over 20mph, a documented daily safety brief, and photographed
ladder setups. **Enforce the weather rule hardest when you are behind
schedule** — that is precisely when it gets broken and precisely when it matters.

### 5. The July inventory bet goes wrong (severe, cash)

Covered in §5. Buy against signed contracts, not forecasts.

### 6. Weather compresses the window (certain, plan for it)

Not a risk — a certainty. It is already priced in as the 85% booking cap and
the Dec 8–15 float. If you book to 100%, you have removed the mitigation and
adopted the risk.

### 7. A national franchise enters your metro (moderate)

They will outspend you on ads and undercut you on price. They cannot beat you
on the January 15 takedown, the 24-Hour Fix, or knowing the customer's roof.
**Do not match their price.** Defend the renewal base — it is the one thing
their marketing budget cannot buy.

## 8. The five numbers to run the company on

| Metric | Season 5 target | Check it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Renewal rate** | 72%+ | Every August. Under 65% = stop growing and fix delivery. |
| **Booked capacity** | ≤ 90% by Oct 15 | Weekly, Sep–Oct. Then stop selling. |
| **Time to first response** | Under 15 minutes | Weekly. The first responder wins ~half the time, and it's free. |
| **Gross margin per job type** | 55%+ blended | Monthly in season. Kill the job types that lose money. |
| **Cost per acquired customer** | Under $250 | By channel, at season end. Kill anything above it. |

If you track nothing else, track the renewal rate. Sections 2, 4, and 7 all
resolve to that one number.

## 9. Beyond $1M — the honest options

$1M with six crews is roughly the ceiling for one metro and one owner. Three
ways past it, in order of risk:

| Path | What it means | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Deepen** — permanent lighting + gutters + washing to the existing list | Same customers, more revenue per customer, year-round crews | **Lowest.** No new acquisition, no new market. Start here. |
| **Widen** — a second metro, second warehouse, second ops manager | Doubles the model | **High.** Everything that's hard at $1M is harder remotely. |
| **Franchise / license the system** | Sell the playbook, not the labor | **Highest.** A different business entirely — you'd be in the training and support business. |

The first row is the one that fits what you've already built: you own a list of
several hundred homeowners who have let you on their roof and trusted you with
their storage. That relationship is the asset. **Sell more to the customers you
already have before you go find new ones.**
